[0] [paper title]: Evaluating External Evidence for LLM-Based Financial News Claim Verification
[1] [Author]: 
[2] [Author]: 
[3] [Normal]: 
[4] [Abstract]: Abstract—Large language models (LLMs) are being used more and more to verify financial claims, but it is still unclear how much of their performance depends on parametric knowledge acquired during pretraining versus evidence supplied at inference time. We present a controlled experimental framework that compares LLM-based financial claim verification under four evidence conditions, using the same claims, the same model, and the same prompt structure: (A) internal-knowledge-only verification with no retrieval; (B1) verification grounded in U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) EDGAR filings; (B2) verification grounded in financial market data; and (B3) verification grounded in the combination of both sources. Claims are drawn from the Fin-Fact benchmark and verified into SUPPORTED, REFUTED, or UNVERIFIABLE labels, with an evaluation protocol covering accuracy, precision, recall, macro-F1, confusion analysis, claim-type breakdown, and failure-mode analysis. We are not proposing a new retrieval-augmented system; instead, we isolate the marginal contribution of specific, authoritative evidence sources under explicit temporal-integrity constraints that prevent future evidence from being used to verify historical claims. We describe the experimental methodology, the evaluation protocol, and a demonstration web application that operationalizes the pipeline, and we outline the evaluation procedure that will be applied once the full experimental run is complete.
[5] [Keywords]: Keywords—financial misinformation detection, large language models, retrieval-augmented generation, parametric knowledge, fact verification, SEC EDGAR, evidence grounding, Fin-Fact
[6] [Heading 1]: Introduction
[7] [Body Text]: Financial news moves markets, and a false or misleading claim about earnings, a regulatory action, or a corporate event can distort investor behavior before anyone gets the chance to correct it. Manual fact-checking simply cannot keep pace with the volume and speed of financial reporting, which is part of why automated financial misinformation detection has drawn growing interest, including LLM-based systems evaluated in dedicated shared tasks [4]–[11].
[8] [Body Text]: A central design question for any LLM-based verifier is where its judgment actually comes from. The model might label a claim SUPPORTED or REFUTED using only the knowledge encoded in its parameters, or it might be given external evidence at inference time, for example through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Prior financial misinformation systems have shown that retrieval-based evidence can be combined effectively with LLM reasoning [9], [13], [14], while a separate line of work studies verification that deliberately withholds retrieval in order to characterize what a model actually “knows” on its own [16]–[18]. What is still missing is a controlled, single-model comparison that isolates the contribution of individual, authoritative evidence sources against the same internal-only baseline, using the same claims and prompt structure.
[9] [Body Text]: We address that gap for the financial domain. The verification model, the prompt template, the claim set, and the output schema are all held fixed, and only the evidence supplied to the model varies, across four conditions: no evidence (internal knowledge only), SEC EDGAR filings, financial market data, and the combination of both. Because the evidence condition is the only variable held unfixed, any resulting performance difference is more plausibly attributable to the evidence source than to confounding changes in the model, the prompting, or the task framing.
[10] [Body Text]: In brief, our contributions center on isolating the marginal effect of specific, authoritative evidence sources under a controlled four-condition design paired with an evaluation protocol that goes beyond aggregate accuracy; Section III lays out these contributions in full. The rest of the paper reviews related work, lays out our research gap, details the methodology, describes the system implementation, and presents the experimental setup and evaluation procedure.
[11] [Heading 1]: Related Work
[12] [Body Text]: Financial misinformation landscape and benchmarks. A recent systematic review maps out the trends, themes, and detection methods used in the fake financial news literature, situating LLM-based detection within a longer history of financial misinformation research [1]. Supervised, non-LLM baselines, such as a BERT-based detector adapted to the financial domain, show why domain-specific adaptation matters for detecting fake financial news [2]. The Fin-Fact benchmark provides professionally annotated financial claims with True/False/Not-Enough-Information labels and explanations, and it is the dataset our controlled comparison is built on [3].
[13] [Body Text]: LLM-based financial misinformation detection systems. Instruction-tuned financial misinformation classifiers such as FMDLlama showed that LLMs could be applied to this task, introducing a dedicated instruction dataset and benchmark [5], which in turn motivated a shared task that formalized financial misinformation detection as an LLM classification and explanation-generation problem, and summarized the participating systems [4]. Within that shared task, instruction-tuned Llama variants were evaluated for direct claim verification [6], a two-stage classify-then-refine framework was proposed [7], multimodal reasoning was used to combine text and image evidence [8], chain-of-thought prompting was combined with retrieved evidence [9], an instruction-tuned GPT-4o-mini baseline achieved competitive performance [10], and the effect of task structuring and metadata on performance was studied directly [11]. Separately, FinDVer evaluated long-context and retrieval-augmented LLMs on explainable claim verification over long, hybrid-content financial documents, finding that even strong models still lag behind human experts [12]. Taken together, this body of work establishes LLM-based financial claim verification as an active and genuinely difficult task, though most of it focuses on optimizing a single system rather than isolating the evidence condition itself.
[14] [Body Text]: RAG and evidence-grounded verification in finance. FinVet combines two retrieval-augmented pipelines with external fact-checking agents and confidence-weighted voting, reporting strong Fin-Fact performance along with source attribution and uncertainty flags [13]. A closely related agentic framework adds traceability and source-authority guardrails, comparing LLM-only, standard RAG, traceable RAG, and full-guardrail RAG conditions directly, though on a small set of Indonesian financial claims [14]. ZAKA-FF combines real-time knowledge retrieval with adversarial refinement to handle time-sensitive and ambiguous financial claims in a zero-shot setting [15]. These papers collectively show that retrieval-augmented financial misinformation detection is already an established design pattern. Our contribution is therefore not the introduction of RAG into this domain, but a controlled comparison of specific, authoritative evidence sources against the same internal-only baseline.
[15] [Body Text]: Internal or parametric-knowledge-only verification. A reference-free counterfactual benchmark studies financial misinformation detection in a setting where models have no access to external fact-checking evidence, exposing the limits of evidence-free verification [16]. A related shared-task system works within this same reference-free setting, relying on fine-tuning and few-shot prompting rather than retrieval [17]. More broadly, outside the financial domain, fact-checking without retrieval has been formalized as a distinct condition that draws on an LLM's internal representations. This gives us a conceptual basis for treating internal-knowledge verification as an explicit, first-class experimental condition, rather than as a degenerate case of retrieval failure [18]. Our Condition A follows that formulation directly.
[16] [Body Text]: Evidence dependence, ablation, and internal–external conflict. Fact-Ablated Evaluation tests whether LLM fact-checkers actually rely on the evidence they are given, by removing cited evidence across rounds and watching whether predictions change. It shows that a model can look accurate while still leaning heavily on parametric knowledge [19]. TruthfulRAG tackles the related problem of factual-level conflicts between retrieved evidence and an LLM's internal knowledge, using knowledge graphs and entropy-based filtering [20], and a separate study shows that where evidence is placed and how long the context is can materially affect retrieval-augmented fact-checking effectiveness [21]. These findings are part of the motivation for our claim-type and failure-mode analysis, since disagreements between the internal-only condition and the evidence-grounded conditions can themselves be informative.
[17] [Body Text]: General RAG evaluation and factuality methodology. RAGChecker offers fine-grained diagnostics that separate retrieval quality from generation quality in RAG pipelines [22], which informs how we plan to assess evidence relevance in the evidence-grounded conditions. Self-RAG shows that training a model to decide when retrieval is worth using, and to critique the retrieved passages, can improve factuality and citation accuracy, while also showing that retrieval quality does not automatically help [23]. RAGTruth contributes a manually annotated corpus for studying unsupported and contradictory claims produced by RAG systems [24], and FactScore breaks generated text down into atomic facts to measure what proportion is supported by reliable sources [25]. None of these methods are specific to finance, but they inform the evaluation protocol we apply to the evidence-grounded conditions.
[18] [Body Text]: Financial evidence and numerical claims. A financial numerical-claim dataset paired with a weak-supervision model studies how numerical claims made in analyst reports and earnings calls relate to subsequent market outcomes [26]. This work underlines that many financial claims are numerical and tied to market movements, which is why we treat market data as a distinct evidence source alongside regulatory filings, rather than lumping financial evidence into a single undifferentiated category.
[19] [Heading 1]: Research Gap and Contributions
[20] [Body Text]: The literature reviewed in Section II shows that retrieval-augmented financial misinformation detection is already an established design pattern [9], [13]–[15], and that at least one prior system already compares an LLM-only condition against retrieval-based conditions [14]. We are therefore not claiming that combining LLMs with retrieved financial evidence is novel, nor that no prior work compares internal-only and retrieval-grounded verification. The reviewed literature provides limited evidence from controlled comparisons that (a) isolate the marginal contribution of specific, authoritative financial evidence sources, namely SEC regulatory filings and market data, both separately and combined, against the same internal-only baseline; (b) enforce an explicit temporal-integrity constraint so that evidence cannot leak information from after the claim's relevant date; and (c) jointly report claim-type and failure-mode analysis together with evidence-dependence diagnostics, in the style of prior evidence-ablation work [19], [22], rather than relying on aggregate accuracy alone.
[21] [Body Text]: Building on this gap, our contributions are: (1) a controlled experimental framework with four evidence conditions, internal-only, SEC-grounded, market-grounded, and combined, applied to the same claims and the same LLM under a fixed prompt structure; (2) an evidence-source-specific design that treats SEC filings and market data as distinct, individually evaluable evidence sources rather than a single retrieval condition; (3) a temporal-integrity protocol that filters evidence against the claim-relevant date before it ever reaches the model; (4) an evaluation protocol spanning accuracy, precision, recall, macro-F1, confusion analysis, claim-type breakdown, model confidence, and evidence-relevance diagnostics; and (5) a demonstration web application that operationalizes the four-condition pipeline for arbitrary financial news input, presented as an implementation artifact that is explicitly separate from the controlled research experiment reported in Sections VI and VII.
[22] [Heading 1]: Methodology
[23] [Heading 2]: Problem Formulation
[24] [Body Text]: Let c denote a financial claim drawn from a fact-checking benchmark. The verification task is to assign c a label y ∈ {SUPPORTED, REFUTED, UNVERIFIABLE}. We define an evidence condition E as the possibly empty set of external evidence passages supplied to the model together with c. The model, prompt template, label set, and decision procedure stay fixed; only E changes across the four conditions A, B1, B2, and B3 defined below. This formulation supports comparing performance differences between conditions while keeping the model, prompt, and task framing constant.
[25] [Heading 2]: Dataset
[26] [Body Text]: Claims are drawn from Fin-Fact, a financial fact-checking benchmark that provides professional fact-checker annotations, textual and visual context, and explanations [3]. The downloaded Fin-Fact release used in this study contains 3,369 records in total; the fixed evaluation sample drawn from these records is described in Section VI. Fin-Fact's native True/False/Not-Enough-Information labels are mapped onto our SUPPORTED/REFUTED/UNVERIFIABLE schema. Consistent with standard evaluation practice and the scientific controls of this study, the gold labels, gold explanations, and gold evidence passages provided by Fin-Fact are withheld from the model input in every condition; the model receives only the claim text and, in the B conditions, evidence retrieved independently of the Fin-Fact annotations.
[27] [Heading 2]: Condition A: Internal-Knowledge Verification
[28] [Body Text]: In Condition A, the model receives only the claim text, the claim date, and the task instructions; no retrieval is performed and no evidence passages are supplied. It has to rely entirely on parametric knowledge acquired during pretraining; this reflects the absence of retrieval at inference time and does not imply the model was never exposed to information about the claim during training, consistent with the reference-free verification framing used in prior work [16]–[18]. This condition acts as the internal-knowledge baseline against which the evidence-grounded conditions are compared.
[29] [Heading 2]: Condition B: Evidence-Grounded Verification
[30] [Body Text]: Condition B is split into three evidence-source variants that share an identical prompt structure and differ only in the evidence supplied. In B1 (SEC evidence), the model receives passages retrieved from SEC EDGAR regulatory filings relevant to the claim. In B2 (market evidence), the model receives financial market data relevant to the claim, such as price or volume history for the instrument and period in question. In B3 (combined evidence), the model receives both the SEC and market evidence from B1 and B2 together. Keeping the model, prompt, and claim identical across B1-B3 isolates the effect of evidence-source composition, consistent with the experimental-control recommendations of prior shared-task analysis [11].
[31] [Heading 2]: Evidence Sources
[32] [Body Text]: SEC EDGAR filings, such as annual reports on Form 10-K, quarterly reports on Form 10-Q, and current reports on Form 8-K, are treated in this study as an authoritative evidence source for claims about corporate disclosures, financial statements, and material events. SEC EDGAR is a public disclosure repository, and we do not claim that the SEC verifies the truth of every filing's contents. Market data, including historical price, volume, and related trading information, is treated as a complementary evidence source suited to claims about market performance and price-linked events, in line with prior findings that many financial claims are numerical and tied to market movements [26]. The two sources differ in authority, latency, and coverage, which is why we evaluate them both separately and in combination rather than assuming that a single retrieval condition would be representative.
[33] [Heading 2]: Temporal Integrity
[34] [Body Text]: Each claim is associated with a claim-relevant date. Evidence retrieved for the B conditions is filtered so that its effective date never postdates the claim-relevant date, which prevents look-ahead bias, that is, using information that would not have been available at the time of the claim to verify it. When no evidence satisfying this constraint is available, the retrieval step returns an explicit empty-evidence result rather than substituting evidence from outside the allowed window. A retrieval or API failure is logged and retried, and it does not by itself cause the model to output UNVERIFIABLE; the label is still determined by the model's verification decision given whatever evidence state is passed to it. This keeps failures of the retrieval pipeline analytically distinct from genuine cases of insufficient evidence.
[35] [Heading 2]: Evaluation Metrics
[36] [Body Text]: Each condition is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and macro-averaged F1 over the three-way label set, together with a confusion matrix to characterize systematic error patterns. Claim-type analysis reports metrics separately for categories such as earnings and revenue, executive or personnel changes, regulatory or compliance actions, and market performance. Failure-mode analysis characterizes recurring error patterns qualitatively, for example over-reliance on parametric knowledge in the face of contradicting evidence, sensitivity to evidence placement consistent with [21], or a failure to update predictions when evidence is withdrawn, informed by the evidence-ablation methodology of [19]. For the evidence-grounded conditions, we also report model confidence and evidence-relevance diagnostics adapted from RAG evaluation methodology [22], to assess whether the retrieved evidence is topically and temporally relevant to the claim being verified.
[37] [Heading 1]: System Implementation
[38] [Body Text]: The controlled experiment described in Section IV is the primary scientific contribution of this work. Alongside it, we built a demonstration web application that runs the same four-condition pipeline on arbitrary, user-supplied financial news rather than only on benchmark claims. The application exists to make the pipeline usable and inspectable; results it produces on arbitrary news input are not benchmark results and are never used as evidence for the comparative findings reported in Sections VII and VIII.
[39] [Heading 2]: News Input
[40] [Body Text]: The application accepts financial news as pasted text, as an uploaded PDF, DOCX, TXT, or HTML file, or as a URL to fetch, and it normalizes every input format to plain text before any downstream processing takes place.
[41] [Heading 2]: Claim Extraction
[42] [Body Text]: Checkable financial claims are then extracted from the normalized text, isolating individual factual assertions, such as reported figures, corporate actions, or regulatory statements, that are suitable for independent verification.
[43] [Heading 2]: Evidence Retrieval
[44] [Body Text]: For each extracted claim, the application retrieves candidate evidence from SEC EDGAR and from market data providers, applying the same temporal-integrity filter described in Section IV-F. This keeps the evidence-eligibility rules consistent between the demonstration pipeline and the controlled experiment.
[45] [Heading 2]: Verification Pipeline
[46] [Body Text]: Each extracted claim is then passed through all four conditions, internal-only, SEC-grounded, market-grounded, and combined, using the same prompt template and label schema as the controlled experiment. This produces a label, a confidence score, and a supporting rationale for each condition.
[47] [Heading 2]: Comparison Dashboard
[48] [Body Text]: For a given claim, the application shows the four conditions' labels, confidences, and rationales side by side, so a user can see where internal-knowledge and evidence-grounded verification agree or disagree, and can inspect the underlying retrieved evidence for the B conditions.
[49] [Heading 1]: Experimental Setup
[50] [Body Text]: Condition A is implemented using Gemini 3.6 Flash (model identifier gemini-3.6-flash), accessed through Google's Gemini API. A direct API smoke test confirmed successful connectivity and response generation with this model before the full experimental run began. No model snapshot date or training cutoff is verified beyond this identifier. The experiment code fixes the prompt and output schema for Condition A, but at the time of writing there is no verified record of an explicitly fixed temperature, maximum output tokens, top-p, top-k, or random seed for generation. These decoding settings are therefore reported as not explicitly fixed or recorded, rather than assumed or invented. Each run is also limited to at most 20 Gemini requests and halts if the free-tier quota is exhausted (HTTP 429, RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED); this is simply a safeguard for managing API rate limits, not an experimental decoding parameter.
[51] [Body Text]: The downloaded Fin-Fact release used in this study (finfact.json) contains 3,369 records, which we verified by direct inspection of the dataset fields. The research experiment draws on a fixed, reproducible stratified sample of 300 claims, sampled with random seed 42 and stratified by the Fin-Fact gold label. The sample contains 133 claims labeled false, 113 labeled true, and 54 labeled Not-Enough-Information (NEI), which map to REFUTED = 133, SUPPORTED = 113, and UNVERIFIABLE = 54 under the schema defined in Section IV-A. We verified that the prepared 300-claim sample contains no duplicate claim identifiers and no duplicate claim texts, and an automated leakage-validation pass found zero violations across the prepared records, confirming that the leakage safeguard described below held for the full sample rather than only by design.
[52] [Body Text]: Sampling was stratified by gold label only; it was not additionally stratified by the four semantic claim-type categories used for the claim-type analysis in Section VII (earnings/revenue, executive/personnel, regulatory/compliance, market performance). Verified per-category counts for those four types are therefore not yet available and are not reported here. Should claim-type analysis be required later, category assignments for the 300 sampled claims will need to be computed separately, using an explicitly documented, deterministic classification scheme.
[53] [Body Text]: Fin-Fact's downloaded JSON release does not include an official train/development/test split field, and we did not introduce an artificial split for this study; the original finfact.json file was left unmodified. Gold labels are kept separate from the model input at all times. For Condition A specifically, the model receives only the claim text, the claim date, and the task instructions; it never receives the Fin-Fact gold label, evidence, justification, issues, evidence or source URLs, author information, or any externally retrieved evidence. We verified this programmatically at the prompt level before the run: an automated check confirmed that the finalized Condition A prompt satisfied its input contract and contained no author-identifying information.
[54] [Body Text]: Conditions B1, B2, and B3 are implemented to additionally supply the same claim and claim date together with independently retrieved SEC EDGAR evidence, market evidence, or their combination, respectively, each subject to the temporal-integrity filter described in Section IV-F: evidence must not postdate the claim-relevant date, and when no eligible evidence is available the evidence state remains explicitly empty rather than being substituted. Retrieval or API failures are logged separately from model verification outcomes and are never automatically converted into an UNVERIFIABLE label. The same model, claim set, prompt structure, label schema, and evaluation procedure are intended to stay constant across all four conditions.
[55] [Body Text]: At the time of writing, data collection for Condition A is still in progress: of the fixed 300-claim sample, 6 predictions have completed successfully and 294 remain outstanding, because the Gemini free-tier quota was exhausted partway through the run. Completed results are preserved by claim identifier, quota failures are not converted into UNVERIFIABLE labels, and the experiment can resume against the same fixed sample once quota is available again. Conditions B1, B2, and B3 have not been run yet. Because only 6 of 300 Condition A predictions are currently available, these partial results are not statistically meaningful and are not reported as an aggregate accuracy, precision, recall, macro-F1, confusion matrix, or aggregate confidence statistic in Section VII. A separate, unrelated 25-claim Gemini pilot carried out earlier in development is likewise excluded from Section VII, since it used a different subset of claims and was never intended as a final research result. Consistent with the distinction drawn in Section V, the demonstration web application and the controlled research experiment remain separate: any output the demonstration application produces on arbitrary, user-supplied news is not used as benchmark ground truth and is not reported as a research accuracy result.

[64] [Heading 1]: Discussion
[65] [Body Text]: The experimental design lets us distinguish between several possible outcomes once results become available. Condition A performing comparably to the B conditions would suggest much of the verifiable content in Fin-Fact claims is already recoverable from parametric knowledge, echoing prior findings that LLMs can perform competitively without retrieval on some fact-checking tasks [18]. A substantial divergence between B1 and B2 would instead indicate that evidence authority and evidence type are not interchangeable for financial verification, arguing against treating “retrieval” as a single design choice, consistent with the source-authority framing of [14]. Should B3 fail to exceed the better of B1 and B2, that would point to evidence redundancy or conflict rather than complementary coverage, consistent with the internal-external conflict phenomena documented in [20].
[66] [Body Text]: The claim-type and failure-mode analysis is meant to reveal whether any aggregate advantage for evidence-grounded conditions is spread evenly across claim categories or concentrated in specific types, such as market-performance claims that would plausibly benefit most from market data. We treat disagreements between Condition A and the B conditions on the same claim as informative failure cases rather than discarding them, since such disagreements can reveal whether the model is anchoring on stale parametric knowledge even when eligible external evidence is available, a pattern documented in general-purpose RAG conflict research [20], [21].
[67] [Heading 1]: Limitations
[68] [Body Text]: This study has several limitations. The experiment is scoped to a single benchmark, Fin-Fact, and a single verification model, so the findings may not transfer to other financial fact-checking datasets or to models with different pretraining data or instruction-tuning procedures. Evidence retrieval quality for the B conditions depends on the coverage and recency of the underlying SEC EDGAR and market-data sources, and imperfect retrieval could understate the real benefit of evidence grounding. The temporal-integrity filter reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk of subtle look-ahead effects, for example when a claim's relevant date is ambiguous or spans a reporting period. Finally, the claim-type categories used for the claim-type breakdown analysis are drawn from the composition of Fin-Fact itself and may not capture the full diversity of financial claims found in unconstrained news text, which the demonstration application processes but does not evaluate against gold labels.
[69] [Heading 1]: Conclusion
[70] [Body Text]: We have presented a controlled experimental framework for comparing internal-knowledge and evidence-grounded LLM verification of financial claims, isolating the marginal contribution of SEC filings, market data, and their combination against a fixed internal-only baseline under matched model, prompt, and claim conditions. Rather than framing retrieval-augmented financial misinformation detection as novel, we position our contribution as a controlled, evidence-source-specific comparison with explicit temporal-integrity constraints, joint claim-type and failure-mode analysis, and a clear separation between the research experiment and the accompanying demonstration application. Future work includes completing the full experimental run described in Section VI, extending the evidence-source comparison to additional authoritative sources such as analyst reports, and checking whether the evidence-dependence patterns we observe generalize to other financial fact-checking benchmarks and models.
